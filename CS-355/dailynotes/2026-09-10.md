# Level point operations

Level point operations&mdash;sometimes just "point operations"&mdash;is **processing each point independent of others.** For images, that means applying an operation to each pixel individually, without taking neighboring pixels into consideration. (We'll look at neighborhood operations next week.)

- Examples:
  - Brightness
  - Contrast
  - Scaling
  - Clipping
  - Negatives
  - Thresholding
  - etc.

```
for all pixel positions x, y:
    out[x, y] = transformation(x, y)
```

## Brightness

$s = r + c$

- $c > 0$ makes the image brighter.
- $c < 0$ makes the image darker.

## Contrast

$s = a r$

- $a > 1$: more contrast.
- $a < 1$: less contrast.

Contrast makes the brighter areas brighter and the darker areas darker.


## Linear operations

$s = ar + c$

- $a$: gain.
- $c$: bias/offset.

## Gamma Responses

Many devices have a nonlinear response:

$$I = V^\gamma + c$$

$V$ is the value your stored in the image or video buffer. The thing to notice here is that the value you save is not directly what is shown. 

If you made a plot with the stored $[0, 255]$ on the $x$-axis and the true $[0, 255]$ look on the $y$-axis, the line would form a "gamma curve" ($\gamma$ is "gamma").

### Gamma correction

To account for gamma response, you do some gamma correction.

$s = r^{1/\gamma}$

## Contrast enhancement

- Contrast enhancement makes differences more distinguishable.
- The process decreases contrast for some ranges of brightness and increases contrasts in other ranges.

Whether a part of the range is being enhanced or reduced depends on the tangent at that point:

- $\text{slope} > 1$: enhancement
- $\text{slope} < 1$: reduction

## Windowing

- Enhancing *one part* of the image range.

e.g., viewing an X-ray (12 bits) on an 8-bit screen. If you know that the range you're interested in is $[500, 2000]$, you can enhance that part of the range.

## Histogram equalization

Trying to automatically optimize contrast by allocating to a contrast distribution (historgram).

Used in post-processing in film all the time, because it can help make things look dramatic 'n shih.

# Color

## The science behind it

- "Color" is the range of wavelengths our eyes can see. Different wavelengths appear as different colors.
- We use red/green/blue to represent our images because our eyes have red, green, and blue cones.
  - We're actually a lot more sensitive to red and green light than we are to blue.

## Intensity vs. brightness 

There is technically a difference between *physical intensity* and *perceptual brightness*. For what we do though, that won't really matter much.

## Color models

For graphics, we need some model to represent color.

> [!NOTE]
> You'll want to memorize a few color models for the test.

The most important model is the RGB model. You can imagine the color model as a cube formed by the $[0, 255]$ range of red, green, and blue axes.

e.g., NTSC-YIQ; CIE LUV and its variations; etc.

### Color channels

Each color component&mdash;red, green, and blue&mdash;is a color channel. You can think of an RGB image as the combination of three color planes, each for a different channel.

### Additive vs. subtract color

- *Subtractive* media *absorbs* light. Mixing all primary colors prouces black.
  - e.g. CMY.
    - (IRL, it's really expensive to mix CMY together when you need black, so printers use CMYK&mdash;K for black.)
- *Additive* media *emits* light. Mixing all primary colors produces white.
  - e.g. RGB.

### Color gamuts

Color gamuts visualize the space of all visible colors using a *chromacity diagram*.

Some important points:

- No three primaries can span the full space of visible colors.
- Different primaries will cover different parts of the space.
- Colors produceable on one device may not be produceable on another.
  - Even if they can, the mix of primaries might be different&mdash;it might require recalibration.

### Luminance and Chromaticity

RGB model is intuitive, but not all that intuitive. We tend to think in brightness (luminance) and hue (chromaticity). The HSI model and its variations (HSV, etc.) uses this idea.
